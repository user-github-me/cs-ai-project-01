2.1 Foundations of Behaviorism
Behaviorism is a foundational school of thought in psychology that emerged in the early 20th century,
championed by pioneers like Ivan Pavlov, John B. Watson, and B.F. Skinner. Before behaviorism,
psychology focused largely on introspective methods—trying to analyze internal, unobservable mental
thoughts and conscious states. Behaviorists radically rejected this, arguing that true scientific inquiry
must focus exclusively on observable behaviors and the external environmental triggers that produce
them.
In simple terms, behaviorism views the brain as a "black box." We cannot reliably read what is inside the
box, but we can measure what goes into it (the Stimulus) and what comes out of it (the Response).
The Stimulus-Response (S-R) Paradigm
At the core of behaviorist theory is the direct link known as the Stimulus-Response (S-R) association:
Stimulus (S): Any detectable change or event in the external environment (e.g., a bright light, a bell
ringing, a touch sensor being pushed).
Response (R): The measurable action or reaction produced by the organism or machine (e.g.,
blinking, moving away, turning left).
Through continuous environmental interaction, living organisms adapt their S-R pairings over time to
survive and thrive.

2.2 Behaviorist AI & Behavior-Based Robotics
In early Artificial Intelligence research (often called Symbolic AI or GOFAI - Good Old-Fashioned AI),
scientists believed that to act intelligently, a robot had to build a complete, detailed 3D internal map of its
surroundings. The robot would gather data, build a complex visual representation, plan a route, and only
then execute a motion step. If the world changed while the robot was calculating, it would fail.
In the late 1980s, researcher Rodney Brooks revolutionized the field by introducing Behaviorist AI (or
Behavior-Based Robotics). Brooks famously declared that "the world is its own best model." Instead of
wasting computational time building slow internal representations, robots should use fast, direct
feedback loops between sensors and actuators.
Situated and Embodied AI
Behaviorist AI hinges on two core principles:
Situatedness: The AI exists directly within its actual physical environment (not in an abstract
simulation) and senses the world in real-time.
Embodiment: The AI possesses a physical body (actuators, wheels, motors) that experiences real-
world physics and direct environmental consequences.

Brooks' Subsumption Architecture
To control robots without a central "brain" map, Brooks developed the Subsumption Architecture. In
this model, robotic intelligence is broken down into autonomous, layered modules. Higher layers can
suppress or subsume lower layers when specific conditions are met, but lower layers continue operating
independently when uninhibited.

Behavior-Based Robotics in Practice: The Vacuum Cleaner Analogy
Consider a modern autonomous robotic vacuum cleaner:
Traditional (Symbolic) approach: Scans the room, calculates geometry, draws a graph of every chair
leg, solves a complex pathing algorithm, and moves. If someone moves a chair, it breaks down.
Behaviorist approach: Uses simple reflex loops.
Rule 0 (Emergency): If bumper sensor touches an object (Stimulus) → Stop, reverse 5cm, rotate 45
degrees (Response).
Rule 1 (Default): Drive forward in a straight line.
Surprisingly complex, intelligent-looking behavior (cleaning an entire floor efficiently) emerges naturally
from the combination of simple rules reacting directly to an environment without any master plan.
2.3 Introduction to Reinforcement Learning (RL)
While reflex loops work well for basic navigation, complex decision-making requires machines that can
learn from experience. Reinforcement Learning (RL) is the computational translation of B.F. Skinner's
operant conditioning. In RL, an autonomous Agent learns how to reach a goal through trial-and-error
interactions with an dynamic Environment.

Core Components of an RL System
State (St): A complete snapshot of the environment's condition at time step t (e.g., the chess board
layout or the robot's GPS coordinates).
Action (At): Any valid move or choice available to the agent in state S (e.g., move forward, turn left,
jump).
Reward (Rt): A numerical scalar feedback signal sent by the environment. Positive values (+10) reward
good decisions; negative values (-5) penalize undesirable outcomes.
Policy (π): The agent's internal strategy or rulebook mapping states to actions (π: S → A). The goal of
RL is to find the optimal policy π*.
Value Function V(s): Estimates the total cumulative reward the agent can expect to collect in the long
run starting from state s.
The Exploration vs. Exploitation Dilemma
A central challenge in reinforcement learning is balancing immediate performance with discovering
better long-term strategies. This is known as the Exploration vs. Exploitation Trade-off:
Exploration: Trying unfamiliar or non-optimal actions to discover new possibilities and gather
information about the environment.
Exploitation: Selecting the best-known action based on current memory to maximize immediate
short-term reward.
Beginner Analogy: Imagine visiting a restaurant. Exploitation is ordering your favorite dish because you
know it's good. Exploration is trying a totally new dish on the menu—it might taste terrible (a penalty),
but you might discover something even better!
AI systems often use an ε-greedy strategy (epsilon-greedy): the agent exploits its known best move
most of the time (e.g., 90% of steps), but randomly explores new actions with a small probability ε (e.g.,
10% of steps).
Model-Free RL: The Q-Learning Algorithm
Q-Learning is one of the most famous model-free RL algorithms. "Model-free" means the agent does not
need to know how the world works beforehand—it learns purely by doing.
The agent maintains a lookup table called a Q-Table storing values Q(s, a), which represent the expected
quality of taking action a in state s. After taking an action, the agent updates its Q-table using the
Bellman Equation update rule:
Q(s, a) ← Q(s, a) + α × [ R + γ × maxa' Q(s', a') − Q(s, a) ]

2.4 Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) Made Simple
How do we represent decision-making mathematically when the world is unpredictable? AI researchers
use a framework called a Markov Decision Process (MDP).
The Board Game Analogy
Think of an MDP as a board game (like Snakes and Ladders or a video game maze):
You stand on a square (State).
You choose a move, like walking forward or jumping (Action).
Rolling a die or slipping on ice means your action might not land perfectly where you intended
(Uncertainty / Probability).
Landing on a gold coin gives points, while falling in a trap loses points (Reward / Penalty).
The Markov Property: "Memoryless" Decisions
An environment has the Markov Property if the future depends only on where you are right now and what
action you take next—not on how you got there.
Example: In Chess, to decide your best next move, you only need to look at the current positions of pieces
on the board. You don't need to know whether a knight moved 5 turns ago or 20 turns ago. The present
state gives you all the information you need.

The 5 Ingredients of an MDP (S, A, P, R, γ)
Every MDP problem is built using five basic ingredients, written together as a tuple (S, A, P, R, γ):

1. S (States): All possible positions or situations the agent can be in (e.g., every tile on a grid).
2. A (Actions): All choices the agent can make (e.g., Up, Down, Left, Right).
3. P(s' | s, a) (Transition Probabilities): The chance that taking action a in state s lands you in new state
s' (accounts for slipping, wind, or luck).
4. R(s, a) (Reward Function): The numerical score given for taking an action in a state.
5. γ (Discount Factor): A weight between 0 and 1 balancing immediate gratification vs. long-term goal
planning.
